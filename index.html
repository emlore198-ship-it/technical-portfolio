Kinematics

FABRIK ↗

A procedural kinematic locomotion solver

Read the complete case study 8-page PDF ↗

Project overview

I built a procedural locomotion system in Blender Geometry Nodes to coordinate multiple articulated legs without animating each joint manually. The system separates persistent foot positions from the inverse-kinematics solve: a phase schedule controls when each foot moves, terrain queries project its proposed position onto the ground, and the resulting targets drive the leg chains. Leg count and joint count parameterize the topology, while stored rest lengths keep the geometric constraints consistent as the system is evaluated.

The core solver implements FABRIK, Forward And Backward Reaching Inverse Kinematics. A backward sweep pins the tip to its target and corrects the joints toward the root; a forward sweep restores the root and corrects the chain outward again. Each correction uses an already-updated neighbor, so the sequence of operations is part of the algorithm. Repeating these paired sweeps reconciles the endpoint conditions with the segment lengths. The complete study traces this process through the equations and annotated node graphs.

The surrounding locomotion system maintains one indexed foot state per leg, staggers the legs through periodic phase offsets, and couples swing lift to the actual foot displacement. Downward raycasts constrain proposed foot positions to the terrain, with a defined fallback when a ray misses. A center derived from the animated feet positions the body, while segment directions and curve normals provide the reference axes needed to orient the rendered parts in three dimensions.

This is a kinematic model, with explicit limits rather than a claim of physical robot control. It does not simulate forces, dynamic balance, actuator behavior, joint limits, or collision. The current implementation also uses a fixed iteration budget and a per-evaluation travel cap. In the complete case study, I explain these boundaries and propose measuring endpoint error, handling unreachable targets, making movement time-step consistent, and adding heading control.

Agent-based simulation

Physarum ↗

A Physarum-inspired pattern formation simulation

Read the complete case study 8-page PDF ↗

Project overview

I built a Physarum-inspired pattern formation simulation in Blender Geometry Nodes in which individual agents interact through a shared scent field. Each agent retains its position and normalized heading, reads nearby scent values, and updates its direction before moving. The environment retains deposits between evaluations, allowing local decisions to influence later behavior. The evolving trails emerge from this feedback loop rather than from a pre-drawn path or an image used to control the simulation.

The agent controller constructs three sensor positions: one ahead and two rotated to either side. It samples the scent field at those locations, preserves forward motion when the forward sample is strongest, and otherwise selects a signed turn toward the stronger side. Fourteen isolated steering checks confirmed attraction, forward preference, and the defined response to ties. A bounded emitter initializes new agents and limits each batch to the remaining capacity; nine population tests checked cases such as partial final batches, disabled emission, and changes to controls during a run.

The scent field is stored as a named attribute on a mesh grid. Its update combines a distance-based footprint around the nearest agent with the previous field, applies retention, and mixes values across neighboring vertices. Sensor distance, movement distance, deposit radius, and grid resolution therefore play different roles in the resulting patterns. A separate shader reads the same attribute for emission and displacement, turning the numerical field into a visible surface. The authored scene, materials, and lighting present that output without becoming part of the simulation feedback.

The portfolio configuration uses a 1024-by-1024 grid and a configurable population cap of 100,000 agents. The documented steering and population checks use smaller probe grids; they are not full-resolution performance benchmarks. The project produces an evolving field, not an extracted shortest path, and includes no learned policy or biologically validated model. The complete case study explains the update ordering, boundary behavior, verification evidence, and potential extensions toward goals, obstacles, graph extraction, and route-cost comparisons.

Computational mathematics

Fractals ↗

From recurrence to geometry

Read the complete case study 8-page PDF ↗

Project overview

I implemented ten mathematical generators in Blender Geometry Nodes and brought them together through one selectable interface. Each generator lives in its own node group, with controls for the relevant construction depth, sampling, and presentation settings. The collection covers Sierpinski triangle, carpet, and pyramid forms; the Koch snowflake and a branching tree; the Barnsley fern; and Mandelbrot, Julia, Burning Ship, and cubic Multibrot sets. Together, they explore three distinct computational approaches to generating structure.

The recursive geometric systems replace previous geometry with smaller copies positioned by translations, rotations, and scale. The construction rule determines both the visible gaps and the growth in geometric complexity: the triangle creates three half-scale copies, the carpet retains eight copies from a three-by-three arrangement, and each Koch refinement replaces a segment with four transformed thirds. The tree extends the same idea through repeated scaled and rotated branches. These systems make transformation order, alignment, and topology central to the implementation.

The Barnsley fern uses probabilistic affine maps rather than explicit geometric replication. A reproducible pseudorandom choice selects one of four transformations for each point at each iteration. The complex-iteration generators use a different representation again: a sampled grid stores the recurrence state and iteration counter as attributes. Complex arithmetic is expanded into scalar operations, escaped samples retain their state, and the counter becomes surface height. Julia, Burning Ship, and cubic Multibrot demonstrate how changing initialization or the recurrence changes the resulting form.

The complete study connects the equations to actual node groups and generated outputs, including count checks for the Sierpinski triangle and inspection of a 250,000-vertex Mandelbrot grid. It also distinguishes exponential geometric growth from the resolution-and-iteration cost of sampled recurrences. Finite-depth geometry approximates self-similarity, while a sample remaining bounded for a fixed iteration budget is not proof of permanent set membership. Runtime performance and numerical error are identified as unbenchmarked, with possible extensions including zoom controls and an exposed Multibrot exponent.
